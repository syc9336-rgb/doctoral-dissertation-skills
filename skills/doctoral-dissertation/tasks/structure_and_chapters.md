# Structure and Chapter Architecture

## First principle

Do not force a fixed chapter count.

The dissertation must contain these **functions**, though they may be distributed across different numbers of chapters:

1. problem definition;
2. theoretical preparation;
3. research design/materials;
4. core evidence;
5. explanation/discussion;
6. integration/contribution;
7. conclusion/boundaries.

## Five-level hierarchy

### Chapter
One major scholarly problem.

### Section
One class of sub-problem.

### Subsection
One central judgment.

### Paragraph
One reasoning move.

### Sentence
One primary information function.

## Chapter test

For each chapter, complete:

> This chapter establishes ______.

If the blank can only be filled with “introduces several topics,” redesign the chapter.

## Section test

Sections at the same level must be parallel or deliberately progressive.

Bad hierarchy:

- Concept definition
- Sample description
- Historical review
- Statistical analysis

Good hierarchy example:

- Construct definition
- Construct distinction
- Mechanism
- Boundary conditions

## Subsection test

Every subsection must be reducible to one mature scholarly statement.

Weak:

> This subsection introduces X.

Strong:

> X should be distinguished from Y because the two judgments rely on different information bases.

## No meta-outline prose in the body

Normally avoid:

- “This chapter first…”
- “The next section will…”
- “The previous section has…”
- “To make the logic clearer…”

Let substantive relationships create transitions.

### Exception

A formal “Dissertation Structure” subsection in the introduction may summarize chapter functions.

Methods may report actual research-stage order.

## Chapter openings

Prefer entering the scholarly issue directly.

Avoid task-list introductions.

Instead of:

> The previous chapter developed the theory. This chapter tests it empirically.

Prefer:

> Whether the proposed relationships receive empirical support depends on whether the focal constructs display stable and distinguishable associations in the observed data.

## Chapter endings

Do not preview the next chapter mechanically.

Avoid:

> These findings lay a foundation for the next chapter.

Prefer:

> The average relationships are now clear, but the evidence does not yet explain why the two outcomes diverge under otherwise similar conditions.

The unresolved scholarly problem creates the transition.

## Default functional blueprints

### Introduction

Typical internal logic:

1. substantive change/problem;
2. narrowing to the scholarly problem;
3. what existing research explains;
4. what remains unresolved;
5. research questions/objectives;
6. scope;
7. overall design;
8. contribution positioning;
9. dissertation structure.

### Literature/Theory

Typical internal logic:

1. field/problem map;
2. concept boundaries;
3. theory;
4. empirical evidence;
5. disagreement and boundary conditions;
6. research gap;
7. model/propositions/hypotheses.

### Methods

Typical internal logic:

1. overall design;
2. data/research object;
3. sampling;
4. instruments/materials;
5. actual procedure;
6. operationalization;
7. quality control;
8. analysis strategy;
9. ethics.

### Results

Typical internal logic:

1. data quality/sample;
2. descriptive evidence;
3. measurement/model quality if applicable;
4. core findings;
5. extensions;
6. robustness/sensitivity;
7. unresolved empirical questions.

### Qualitative/Explanatory Study

Typical internal logic:

1. explanatory question;
2. participants/material;
3. analysis procedure;
4. themes/findings;
5. cross-theme relationships;
6. relation to prior evidence.

### Integration/Discussion

Typical internal logic:

1. answer RQs across studies;
2. explain mechanisms;
3. integrate evidence;
4. theoretical advancement;
5. disciplinary/practical translation;
6. applicability boundaries.

### Conclusion

Typical internal logic:

1. concise study overview;
2. conclusions by RQ;
3. theoretical contribution;
4. methodological contribution;
5. disciplinary/practical contribution;
6. limitations;
7. future research.

## Alternative architectures

Use when appropriate:

- 5-chapter conventional model;
- multi-study model;
- design/practice model;
- article-based thesis model;
- qualitative monograph model.

Never add a “stimulus/material development” chapter unless the actual project needs one.
