# Write Chapters, Sections, and Paragraphs

## Before writing a subsection

Privately answer four questions:

1. What does this subsection need to establish?
2. What is the mature answer?
3. What evidence supports it?
4. What scholarly issue follows from it?

Do not print these planning questions into the thesis.

## Skeleton pass

Before full prose, draft:

- chapter function;
- section functions;
- one central judgment per subsection;
- one topic sentence per planned paragraph.

## Evidence pass

Attach each paragraph to:

- literature;
- data;
- figure/table;
- participant evidence;
- documented research procedure.

## Explanation pass

For important claims, add:

- mechanism;
- comparison;
- theoretical meaning;
- conditions.

## Paragraph rule

A paragraph should usually contain:

1. central claim;
2. evidence;
3. interpretation;
4. substantive link.

This is a reasoning architecture, not a rigid four-sentence template.

## Avoid paragraph fragmentation

Do not create many 1–2 sentence paragraphs simply to simulate structure.

If “first / second / third” items are short and belong to one reasoning unit:

- keep them together;
- or use a deliberate list;
- or create real parallel subsections.

## Avoid overlong paragraphs

If one paragraph contains multiple independent claims, split by reasoning function, not by arbitrary length.

## Paragraph openings

Prefer a substantive claim.

Weak:

> Many scholars have studied this issue.

Better:

> Existing evidence explains the relationship under stable conditions, but the same mechanism becomes less consistent when the information source itself is under evaluation.

## Paragraph endings

Use the ending to:

- conclude locally;
- identify a substantive unresolved issue;
- establish the next conceptual relationship.

Avoid empty transition sentences such as:

> Based on the above, the next part is discussed below.

## Body text should not expose author operations

Delete or rewrite:

- “to facilitate understanding”;
- “to make the logic clearer”;
- “the following section discusses”;
- “we next derive”;
- “the original plan was”;
- “after several attempts”;
- “to address reviewers’ concerns.”

## Strong-but-warranted prose

If evidence supports a claim, say it directly.

Weak:

> This finding may to some extent provide some insight into X.

Stronger:

> This finding distinguishes X from Y and shows that the two outcomes rely on different judgment conditions.

Do not use assertiveness to exceed the evidence, but do not weaken supported conclusions by habit.
