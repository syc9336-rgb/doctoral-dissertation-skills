# Language and Style Reference

## Target voice

The dissertation should be:

- scholarly;
- natural;
- precise;
- concrete;
- controlled;
- readable;
- confident where warranted;
- limited where necessary.

It should not sound:

- promotional;
- bureaucratic;
- defensive;
- machine-generated;
- like reviewer correspondence;
- like a research diary.

## Finished-state principle

The thesis body presents mature research knowledge.

Readers should see:

- the problem;
- the theoretical relationship;
- the evidence;
- the finding;
- the interpretation;
- the contribution.

They should normally not see:

- how the author thought;
- how the draft changed;
- what reviewers might object to;
- how the author “derived” the next point;
- why a sentence was inserted.

## One sentence, one primary relation

Split sentences that simultaneously contain:

- background;
- theory;
- result;
- caveat;
- contribution.

Especially audit abstracts, chapter openings, chapter summaries, and theoretical contribution sections.

## Prefer active analytical verbs

Use:

- compare;
- examine;
- identify;
- distinguish;
- test;
- estimate;
- explain;
- integrate;
- reveal;
- extend;
- qualify;
- revise.

Avoid heavy nominalization.

Weak:

> An analysis of the influence mechanism was conducted.

Better:

> The study examined how X was associated with Y.

## Avoid formulaic AI transitions

Use sparingly:

- first;
- second;
- moreover;
- in addition;
- on this basis;
- in summary;
- it is worth noting;
- obviously.

Let substantive relations create continuity.

## Avoid vague academic padding

Audit:

- important significance;
- high-quality development;
- empowerment;
- optimization;
- innovation;
- scientific;
- systematic.

Ask:

> What exactly is improved, through what mechanism, and according to what evidence?

## Do not rotate core terminology for stylistic variety

Once a construct is defined, use the same term consistently.

Precision outranks lexical variety.

## Hedge discipline

Do not stack:

> may + to some extent + preliminarily + suggest

Use one hedge only when evidence requires it.

If uncertainty is already expressed through CI/effect size/design limitations, do not redundantly weaken the sentence.

## Strong scholarly judgment

When supported, prefer:

- “The findings show…”
- “The analysis identifies…”
- “This distinction extends…”
- “The evidence supports…”
- “The study differentiates…”

rather than:

> “may provide some reference.”

## No automatic self-weakening in conclusions

Do not write:

> “This study only preliminarily…”

unless the evidence is truly preliminary.

State what the study established, then place genuine limitations in the limitations section.
